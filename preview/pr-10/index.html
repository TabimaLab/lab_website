How can understudided fungi teach us about life

The Tabima Lab at Clark University studies the genomics, ecology and evolution of microscopic fungi associated to animal and plant hosts. From Basidiobolus, a fungus that lives in frog guts, leaf litter, soil and pond sediment, to plant pathogens, microbiomes and host-fungi interactions, our goal is to explore how these organisms can teach us the novelties of life.

See our research Join the lab

Fungi are widely known as molds and mushrooms, but most of the fungal world is microscopic, and the majority of it has never been described. Our group studies the diversity, genomics, evolution and ecology of these small fungi and the hosts and ecosystems they live in.

We use the genus Basidiobolus as our model. It lets us ask how fungi and hosts evolve together, what secondary metabolism does for a fungus, how fungal populations change over time, and how fungi and bacteria share the gut.

Life cycle plasticity and genome evolution in Basidiobolus

We study how Basidiobolus moves among vertebrate guts, leaf litter, soil, insects and aquatic sediments. With whole-genome sequencing, comparative genomics and secondary metabolite profiling, we test whether specialists and generalists differ in genome architecture and in their chemical defenses.

Read the paper

Microbiomes and host–fungal interactions in amphibians

We sample skin and gut fungal communities across amphibian species to learn how host ecology, urbanization and infection pressure shape microbial assembly. Field studies in New England, the Pacific Northwest and Colombia are paired with culture-based experiments that uncover new fungal diversity.

Learn more

Antifungal resistance in emerging Zoopagomycota

We examine intrinsic and induced resistance to common antifungals, including how biosynthetic gene clusters are regulated and which molecular defenses switch on under drug challenge. We combine transcriptomics, metabolomics and functional assays, in collaboration with partners at various other universities.

Learn more

Urbanization and waterway microbial ecology

We investigate how land use shapes microbial diversity in sediment, water and wildlife along the Tatnuck Brook watershed and other regional systems. High-throughput sequencing and ecological modeling help us identify what drives fungal community change, with uses for environmental monitoring.
